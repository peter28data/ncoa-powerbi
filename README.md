## Executive Overview: Aging in America — Key Takeaways

**1. This is a Multi-Condition population, not a single-disease one.**

93% of adults 65+ carry at least one chronic condition, and 80% carry two or more. Any segmentation or care model built around a single flagship condition (e.g., diabetes management) will miss the majority of this population, who are managing several conditions simultaneously. Program design should default to bundled, not siloed, interventions.

**2. Risk doesn't plateau at 65 — it accelerates within the cohort.**

Arthritis prevalence jumps from 44% (65–74) to 53.9% (75+); heart disease is concentrated even more heavily in the 75+ group (24.1%). Treating "65+" as one segment obscures a real inflection point around 75. Resource allocation and outreach should be cohort-differentiated, not age-threshold-differentiated.

**3. Falls are the single largest preventable cost line in this dataset.**

One in four adults 65+ falls annually (~14M people), driving $80B in non-fatal-fall healthcare costs in a single year. Relative to the other conditions tracked here, falls are unique in being both high-cost and demonstrably modifiable — which the program data below confirms.

**4. The falls-prevention program shows real, but partial, impact — and that nuance matters.**

"A lot" fear-of-falling dropped from 13% to 8% (a ~38% relative reduction) after program participation, and "a little" fear rose from 39% to 47%. Read together, this isn't fear disappearing — it's fear *de-intensifying*: people are moving from high anxiety to manageable caution, not to none. That's a more credible, defensible ROI story than "fear eliminated," and it's the right framing for stakeholder reporting.

**5. The population base this all sits on is about to grow 54%.**

57.8M today scaling to ~89M by 2060 means every pressure point above — chronic disease burden, falls cost, care capacity — grows with it if nothing changes. The falls-prevention signal in takeaway #4 is the most actionable lever in the dataset *precisely because* it's the one place we have before/after evidence that intervention moves the number.

**Bottom line:** 

The data makes the strongest case for prioritizing falls-prevention investment now — it's the highest-cost, most-preventable, and only line item with measured program impact — while cohort-splitting (65–74 vs. 75+) should replace blanket 65+ targeting across chronic disease programs.

**One caveat carried from the data itself:** 

NCOA publishes aggregate national statistics only — no state, regional, or longitudinal breakdowns. These takeaways are directionally sound but shouldn't be used for geographic or year-over-year resourcing decisions without a supplementary primary data source.
